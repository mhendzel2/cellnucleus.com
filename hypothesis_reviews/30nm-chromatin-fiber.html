30 nm Chromatin Fiber In Vivo Debate

Does the 30 nm solenoid or zigzag fiber exist as a stable, prevalent structure in the nuclei of living cells?

Scientific text and reference review

Checked 3 October 2026. The original focused review appraised its statements against six primary studies below; the dated MINFLUX addition records newer evidence, including their specimen and method limits. This is a focused evidence review, not an exhaustive literature survey or independent replication. Linked long-form documents and figure reuse rights require separate review.

What claim is being tested?

The strong claim is that a regular, ordered fiber about 30 nm wide is an obligatory, prevalent folding intermediate in mammalian chromosomes. This must be distinguished from the narrower observation that selected nucleosome arrays can form ordered fibers. The studies below test different preparations and cellular regions; they cannot be treated as measurements of a single interchangeable chromatin state. [1] [3] [5] [6]

Ordered fibers under defined conditions

Reconstituted arrays establish physical possibility

Robinson et al. assembled long arrays of the 601 nucleosome-positioning sequence with chicken erythrocyte histone octamers and linker histone H5, then folded them in 1.6 mM MgCl2. Electron microscopy found compact fibers; their measured diameters depended on the repeat-length class. These were engineered arrays examined by negative-stain and selected cryo-EM measurements, rather than a survey of intact living nuclei. [1]

Repeat length and linker histone change the outcome

Routh et al. compared 167-bp and 197-bp repeat arrays folded in 1.6 mM MgCl2. With saturating H5, negative-stain EM measured mean diameters of 21.3 nm (SD 3.0) and 34.3 nm (SD 2.7), respectively. The longer-repeat array compacted cooperatively as H5 occupancy increased. Those numbers describe these reconstituted, fixed specimens; they are not universal nuclear fiber dimensions. [2]

A biological positive control

Nishino et al. detected 30-nm structures by cryo-EM and a corresponding scattering peak in isolated chicken erythrocyte nuclei. This specialized preparation is a counterexample to an absolute claim that ordered fibers never occur in biological material. It does not establish their prevalence in mammalian nuclei. [3]

Evidence against an obligatory mammalian fiber

Mitotic HeLa chromosomes: Nishino et al. (2012)

The authors examined isolated mitotic HeLa chromosomes using cryo-EM of high-pressure-frozen vitreous sections, small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS), and ultra-small-angle X-ray scattering (USAXS). An initial 30-nm SAXS peak was traced to chromosome-associated ribosome aggregates and disappeared after their removal. The combined measurements found no notable regular structure above 11 nm over the tested chromosome-scale range. This was not cryo-electron tomography of intact interphase nuclei. Isolation, washing and cryosectioning remain preparation boundaries, and the authors explicitly considered possible loose or minority fiber arrangements. [3]

Interphase and mitotic cells: Ou et al. (2017)

ChromEMT combines DNA-selective DRAQ5/DAB labeling with multitilt electron tomography. In sampled human small-airway epithelial cells and mitotic U2OS cells, the authors reported disordered chromatin chains with diameters spanning 5–24 nm and variable packing, without a regular 30-nm hierarchy. The specimens were chemically fixed and stained, rather than imaged by native cryo-ET. Their chain-thickness estimates should not be equated directly with the width of a larger nucleosome arrangement in another imaging method. [4]

Native-cell cryo-ET: heterogeneous local organization

CEM T-lymphoblasts: Hou et al. (2023)

Cryo-FIB milling and cryo-ET resolved chromatin in frozen-hydrated interphase human CEM T-lymphoblasts. In heterochromatin near the nuclear envelope, the authors observed variable 20–50-nm-wide arrangements and relaxed zigzags, rather than uniform 30-nm one-start or two-start filaments. Some short local arrangements resembled compact reconstituted arrays. Population analysis used 6,790 selected nucleosome particles from five tomograms; linker paths were partly predicted from nucleosome positions, orientations and resolved linker/H1 density. This limited sampling does not characterize every genomic region or cell type, and a frozen tomogram does not measure live-cell dynamics. [5]

Primary resting CD4+ T cells: Kreysing et al. (2026)

At the nuclear periphery of primary resting human T cells, cryo-ET, template matching, subtomogram averaging and linker-DNA prediction identified an elongated, heterogeneous arrangement with a characteristic width of approximately 37 nm. It lacked the ordered architecture of a canonical 30-nm fiber. Linker connectivity was inferred with a physics-based algorithm and checked against image density; molecular-dynamics simulations explored selected modeled arrangements. The width describes that organization, not a new universal fiber class. The authors emphasize peripheral heterochromatin sampling, incomplete particle detection and locally uncertain linker predictions. Active chromatin and dynamics were outside the direct observations. [6]

Interpretation and remaining scientific limits

Taken together, the cellular studies argue against a regular 30-nm fiber as an obligatory folding intermediate in the mammalian specimens examined. Reconstitution and chicken erythrocyte controls support narrower, context-dependent ordered structures. This synthesis does not prove that every ordered local array is absent, transient, or an extraction artifact. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5] [6]

These structure measurements alone do not select a unique mechanism for chromatin compaction or establish effects on transcription. Demonstrating native prevalence requires matched cell states, sampled regions and preparation methods; establishing dynamics requires time-resolved measurements. The present evidence review therefore offers a qualitative, specimen-bounded conclusion without numerical support scores. [5] [6]

Conceptual illustration

New live-cell MINFLUX evidence — 12 October 2026

Xie and colleagues reported some approximately 30-nm fiber-like DNA localization clouds in living U2OS nuclei using MINFLUX and an AT-preferring Hoechst-derived dye. The structures had variable widths; more extensive geometry measurements came from fixed cells. The authors note that the probe samples selected DNA, moving regions can be missed, and dye binding might stabilize structures. This is evidence for selected fiber-like domains, with neither genome-wide prevalence nor a canonical ordered nucleosome-and-linker topology established by the dye localizations. Xie et al. (online 2025; 2026 issue).

The strong claim tested here remains an obligatory, prevalent and regularly ordered mammalian folding intermediate. The MINFLUX paper adds a positive observation of selected fiber-like structures; its sampling and structural readout do not settle that stronger claim. A claim that no fiber-like domain can ever occur in living mammalian cells would now be too broad. Xie et al. (online 2025; 2026 issue).
