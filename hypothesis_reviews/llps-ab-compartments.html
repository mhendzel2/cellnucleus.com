A/B Compartments: Polymer Segregation and Liquid-Phase Claims

An appraisal of what contact compartments, polymer simulations and chromatin material measurements establish about phase-separation hypotheses.

Scientific text and reference appraisal

Each retained scientific assertion in this focused HTML version was appraised against the named primary evidence at the access depth stated below. Full-article and abstract-only evidence are distinguished. Earlier unsupported or unappraised broad sections were withdrawn. Linked documents, exhaustive literature coverage and independent experimental replication remain separate checks.

A contact compartment is a pattern in the measured data

Rao et al. identified compartment patterns from population in situ Hi-C and related those patterns to chromatin states. A/B labels describe preferential contact relationships and biological enrichments in the sampled cells. The contact pattern does not itself measure viscosity, a protein concentration boundary or a uniquely shaped droplet, and should not be treated as a direct material-state measurement. [1] Evidence and interpretation: Rao et al. (2014).

Polymer microphase separation is already a phase-separation model

Nuebler et al. modeled connected A- and B-type chromatin segments with preferential B-B attraction. The model generated microphase segregation, and the authors emphasized that chain connectivity constrains the spatial patterns. They also distinguished phase segregation from polymer collapse: their selected weak attraction did not require a dense collapsed B-polymer state. Describing this as a model without any phase separation would misstate its premise. [2] Evidence and interpretation: Nuebler et al. (2018).

Active extrusion can change the compartment pattern

In the Nuebler simulations, loop extrusion counteracted segregation, and changing extrusion or barrier assumptions reproduced several contact-pattern effects seen in published cohesin-loading, CTCF and WAPL perturbations. The authors explicitly left the microscopic compartment interaction mechanism uncertain. Agreement with these perturbation patterns therefore supports the modeled interplay, while not identifying one unique molecular driver or proving a protein-droplet mechanism. [2] Evidence and interpretation: Nuebler et al. (2018).

Radial position adds a related but distinct constraint

The inspected primary abstract of Falk et al. combines inverted rod-nucleus Hi-C, microscopy and polymer simulations. It reports that heterochromatin attractions supported segregation and concentric organization, while added lamina interactions recovered conventional organization in the model. This helps distinguish chromatin-chromatin and chromatin-envelope effects, but the abstract-level evidence does not resolve the full molecular identity or liquid properties of the interacting components. [3] Evidence and interpretation: Falk et al. (2019).

Material measurements must retain their own biological scope

Gibson et al. found liquid-like droplet behavior in reconstituted nucleosome arrays. Erdel et al., in contrast, found no separate liquid HP1 pool in the mouse fibroblast chromocenters they examined and favored a collapsed-polymer interpretation. These studies concern different specimens and observables. Together they show why a reconstituted capacity and a native-cell material interpretation cannot be substituted for a genome-wide contact-compartment mechanism. [4] [5] Evidence and interpretation: Rao et al. (2014); Nuebler et al. (2018); Falk et al. (2019); Gibson et al. (2019); Erdel et al. (2020).

The hypothesis requires a specified mechanism and test

The checked evidence supports compartmental segregation and several possible physical accounts, with stronger restrictions than a single universal LLPS verdict. A model should specify the chromatin interactions, chain connectivity, activity and material observable it addresses. Polymer microphase separation and protein-liquid formation are not interchangeable claims; the reviewed studies do not establish that all A/B compartments are protein droplets or that all phase-separation mechanisms are excluded. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5] Evidence and interpretation: Gibson et al. (2019); Erdel et al. (2020).

New chromatin experiments add distinct scales

Evidence update · 12 October 2026

Zhou and colleagues combine cryo-electron tomography with simulations of reconstituted chromatin to relate linker-dependent nucleosome organization to condensate networks and material behavior. Goel and colleagues use capture Micro-C in mouse erythroid cells to show mitotic microcompartments with regulation partly distinct from A/B patterns. Reconstituted material properties, focal contacts and A/B compartments remain different observables. Multiscale structure of chromatin condensates explains phase separation and material properties (2025); Dynamics of microcompartment formation at the mitosis-to-G1 transition (2025).
