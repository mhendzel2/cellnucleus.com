Beyond Liquid Droplets: Phases, Networks, and Bridging

Compare phase coexistence, network mechanics, and chromatin bridging, with body-specific examples and limits of imaging and recovery assays.

Three physical questions

This overview distinguishes phase coexistence, molecular connectivity, and material response. Feric et al. (2016) measured different properties of nucleolar subcompartments; Li et al. (2012) linked demixing with multivalent network assembly; Brackley et al. (2013) modeled clustering through DNA bridges. These examples motivate separate measurements rather than a universal material-state label. Feric et al. (2016). Li et al. (2012). Brackley et al. (2013).

Coexisting phases with different properties

Feric et al. (2016) studied amplified nucleoli in Xenopus laevis oocytes and mixtures of purified NPM1, FIB1, and rRNA. In the cellular system, imaging and fusion analyses distinguished the NPM1-rich granular component from FIB1-rich dense fibrillar regions. Reconstitution produced two coexisting protein-rich phases with layered organization. These observations support a multiphase explanation in the tested system; reconstitution did not reproduce every nucleolar component.

Fusion dynamics, fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP), and probe-particle microrheology distinguished their material responses. Purified NPM1 droplets behaved more like viscous liquids, while FIB1 droplets showed viscoelasticity and recovery that declined with age. Material interpretation therefore depends on the measured component and timescale. Feric et al. (2016).

Networks and polymer bridges

Li et al. (2012) observed demixing driven by multivalent SH3/proline-rich-motif interactions and supported a likely coupled sol–gel transition through scattering and photobleaching measurements. The “likely” qualification matters: molecular-network formation and a macroscopic phase transition are related questions, with different measurements.

Brackley et al. (2013) used Brownian-dynamics simulations of bead-and-spring DNA or chromatin with reversibly binding proteins. Bridging produced clustering without an explicit attractive protein–protein interaction; monovalent controls showed little clustering. This demonstrates a possible polymer-mediated mechanism under specified simulation assumptions. Finite simulation times, chosen interactions, and initial conformations limit inference about an actual nuclear focus.

Interpret imaging and recovery with a model

Fluorescence recovery tracks labeled molecules, while deformation and probe motion interrogate material response. Feric et al. (2016) combined these readouts to distinguish NPM1 and FIB1 behavior. Their combined evidence supports interpreting recovery together with complementary measurements.

For material-state interpretation, combine recovery with measurements that interrogate deformation or motion over stated timescales. This is a methodological recommendation supported by Feric’s combined fusion, FRAP, and microrheology experiments. Incomplete FRAP recovery alone is not a measurement of elastic modulus or proof of a permanent solid (Feric et al. (2016)).

A measured example of active regulation

Feric et al. (2016) depleted ATP in oocytes and mammalian cells: FIB1 recovery slowed or decreased, whereas NPM1 recovery changed comparatively little. In vitro FIB1 droplets also aged toward slower, less complete recovery. These results support active maintenance of dynamics in that component. ATP sensitivity is a perturbation result to interpret with composition and mechanics; it does not, by itself, assign the assembly mechanism.

Evidence assessment

The selected studies support coexisting phases, coupled multivalent networks, and a simulated bridging mechanism in specified systems. They justify measuring composition, connectivity, and relaxation separately. They do not establish a solid core in every nuclear body or the material state of every stress granule, P body, or HP1 assembly. Feric et al. (2016). Li et al. (2012). Brackley et al. (2013).

Evidence checklist

Coexistence: identify the components and measure phase composition; Feric’s reconstitution tested NPM1/FIB1/rRNA rather than the complete nucleolus (Feric et al. (2016)).

Connectivity: test multivalency and association; Li’s data supported a likely network transition, while Brackley’s bridging result was computational (Li et al. (2012); Brackley et al. (2013)).

Material response: state length, time, and perturbation conditions and combine complementary measurements. Feric’s fusion and microrheology complemented FRAP (Feric et al. (2016)).

A recent RNA-processing perturbation tests architectural causation

Quinodoz and colleagues spatially mapped rRNA maturation and engineered nucleoli with processing defects. Disrupting small-subunit processing reordered nucleolar layers and blocked normal rRNA outflux. This provides a substrate-linked perturbation of multiphase architecture, complementary to earlier material measurements. It does not establish that every nuclear body contains a permanent solid core or that all RNA-protein assemblies use the same phase mechanism. Quinodoz et al. (2025).
