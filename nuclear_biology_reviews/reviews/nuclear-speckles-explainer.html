Nuclear Speckles

Nuclear speckles gather RNA-processing proteins. Scientists test their roles by watching genes and carefully changing the proteins that hold speckles together.

A gathering place inside the nucleus

A speckle has no surrounding membrane. Many proteins involved in handling RNA gather there. In human cells grown in the laboratory, reducing two proteins called SON and SRRM2 dispersed other speckle proteins. That experiment helped identify components that hold these bodies together. [1]

Watching genes near speckles

One study followed heat-shock genes in human and hamster cells. Genes close to speckles accumulated more RNA, and live movies helped establish which change came first. More RNA can mean that more was made, less was destroyed, or both. It does not automatically mean faster joining of RNA pieces. [2]

Testing how RNA pieces are joined

In a 2026 study, researchers rapidly removed SON and SRRM2 from engineered human cells. RNA processing became less accurate for a particular group of genes, rich in the DNA letters G and C and containing short introns. The result supports a role in joining RNA pieces for that group; it does not establish the same dependence for every gene. [3]

Removing these proteins changes their own activities as well as the speckles. Further experiments can help distinguish those effects. A visible dot under a microscope, by itself, cannot settle that question. [1] [3]
