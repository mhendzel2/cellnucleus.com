Scientific text and reference appraisal

Each retained scientific assertion in this focused HTML version was appraised against the named primary evidence at the access depth stated below. Full-article and abstract-only evidence are distinguished. Earlier unsupported or unappraised broad sections were withdrawn. Linked documents, exhaustive literature coverage and independent experimental replication remain separate checks.

Gene discovery identified one major cause, rather than all cases

The primary abstract of Ng et al. reports exome discovery and follow-up sequencing that linked mutations in MLL2, the histone-methyltransferase gene named in that report, to Kabuki syndrome. The study included both newly arising and inherited variants in informative families. The result supports gene attribution in the examined cohorts; it does not certify every variant in that gene, explain every affected person or establish a single complete cellular mechanism. [1]

A second gene finding broadened the genetic explanation

Lederer et al. reported three Kabuki-syndrome individuals with de novo partial or complete deletions of KDM6A. The inspected primary abstract identifies KDM6A as a histone demethylase interacting with MLL2 and also reports skewed X-inactivation. This case series supports genetic heterogeneity; it does not show that methyltransferase and demethylase loss have identical effects at every chromatin site or isolate all consequences of a gene deletion. [2]

A mouse observation connects marks and phenotype within a model

The primary abstract of Bjornsson et al. describes a heterozygous Kmt2d mouse model with reduced dentate-gyrus H3K4me3 associated with reduced neurogenesis and hippocampal memory defects. These baseline observations connect a chromatin-related genotype, a histone mark and a phenotype in that model. They do not alone establish the corresponding causal sequence in every human patient. Pharmacologic results in the paper are outside this retained appraisal. [3]

DNA-methylation profiles add a different type of evidence

Sadikovic et al. report an assessment of genome-wide DNA-methylation profiles from peripheral-blood DNA in validation and clinical-referral cohorts containing rare neurodevelopmental disorders and controls. Profiles were compared with existing reference episignature classes. The inspected article reports concordant classifications for many validation samples and positive, negative or inconclusive outcomes among referred subjects. A reference-class resemblance is a measured downstream signature, rather than direct identification of every disrupted chromatin interaction. [4]

The inspected article reports limits from reference coverage, mosaicism, variant location and the existence of more than one signature for some genes. The article explicitly states that a negative result is not an absolute rule-out of pathogenicity. Conversely, a positive signature can guide attribution without establishing a complete mechanism, prognosis or treatment response. These limits should accompany claims about episignatures rather than treating the classifier as universally decisive. [4]

The inspected article identifies a linked correction (DOI 10.1038/s41436-021-01130-z). The correction’s full text was not appraised, so the affected material and any impact on the reported classifications or conclusions remain unverified here. The classifier findings above are attributed to the inspected article; this review does not independently confirm that they are unaffected by the correction. Linked correction [4]

Keep the evidence levels separate

Gene-discovery findings, preclinical mark-phenotype associations and blood methylation classification answer different questions. Together they motivate study of chromatin-related developmental disease, while leaving many variant-specific and tissue-specific mechanisms unresolved. The checked evidence does not supply a complete catalogue of chromatinopathies, a universal connection to cancer or an established therapy for these disorders. [1] [2] [3] [4]
