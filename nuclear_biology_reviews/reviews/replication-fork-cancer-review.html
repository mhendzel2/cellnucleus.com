Preserving newly synthesized DNA

Schlacher and colleagues used single-molecule DNA fibers to examine nascent tracks after fork stalling. Tracks were degraded in BRCA2-deficient models but remained stable in the corresponding proficient cells. [schlacher2011] A 2011 erratum is indexed for this study. Its full correction text was not appraised; the affected material and impact on this finding remain unverified. [indexed correction]

Their BRCA2 analysis separated a RAD51-stabilization-associated fork-protection function from homology-directed double-strand-break repair. MRE11-associated degradation and chromosome-aberration endpoints supported protection of nascent DNA as a distinct contribution to genome integrity. [schlacher2011]

Track preservation measures DNA stability under the tested stress. It does not by itself resolve every stalled-fork structure or demonstrate that a break has been repaired. [schlacher2011]

Replication demand and nucleotide supply

Bester and colleagues examined newly transformed cells with uncoordinated proliferation-pathway activation. Reduced nucleotide pools accompanied altered replication dynamics and DNA-damage endpoints. [bester2011]

The authors interpreted their experimental comparisons as a mismatch between replication demand and nucleotide supply. This is a mechanism supported in those models, rather than proof that nucleotide shortage initiates every human cancer. [bester2011]

Keeping the cancer inference bounded

The studies address different failure modes: loss of nascent-DNA protection and inadequate substrate supply during proliferation. Their convergence on genome instability does not make the mechanisms interchangeable or establish a clinical diagnostic or treatment rule. [schlacher2011] [bester2011]

Primary Abstracts and bibliographic identities were inspected. Exact cell-line composition, detailed quantitative controls and patient-tumor prevalence were not established at that access depth. [schlacher2011] [bester2011]
