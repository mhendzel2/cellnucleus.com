Laminopathies

Human genetic studies link particular LMNA variants to disease; separate cell experiments test what loss of lamin A/C does to nuclear mechanics.

Start with the gene and the protein network

LMNA encodes lamins A and C, proteins associated with the nuclear lamina, a network beneath the inner nuclear membrane. Laminopathies include diseases linked to alterations of these proteins. To understand a particular disorder, distinguish its human genetic evidence from experiments that remove lamin A/C altogether. [1] [3]

Human genetics: muscular dystrophy

Bonne and colleagues found four LMNA variants that co-segregated with autosomal dominant Emery-Dreifuss muscular dystrophy in five families. The disorder involves muscle weakness and can involve cardiac conduction disease. The result connects defined variants to a human phenotype; it does not mean that every LMNA change causes this disorder or has the same cellular effect. [1]

Human genetics: progeria and RNA splicing

Eriksson and colleagues identified the same newly arisen LMNA substitution in 18 of 20 classical Hutchinson-Gilford progeria cases. Although the substitution preserves the encoded amino acid, it activates an abnormal RNA splice site, producing lamin A missing 50 amino acids. Patient fibroblast staining also showed nuclear-envelope abnormalities. HGPS has premature-ageing-like features; these findings do not establish that ordinary ageing is the same process. [2]

A mechanical test uses a different model

Lammerding and colleagues stretched mouse embryonic fibroblasts lacking lamin A/C and compared them with wild-type cells. The deficient nuclei deformed more, and the cells showed reduced viability under mechanical strain and altered responses of mechanically stimulated genes. This demonstrates consequences of deficiency in that model. A complete mouse knockout is not equivalent to a particular human disease variant, so mechanical weakness is not a universal explanation of laminopathies. [3]

Primary studies behind this guide

  3. Lammerding et al. (2004), Journal of Clinical Investigation 113:370–378. Lamin A/C deficiency causes defective nuclear mechanics and mechanotransduction. DOI: 10.1172/JCI19670. Primary research.
