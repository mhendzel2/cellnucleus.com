Overview

Nuclear-envelope studies resolve different aspects of the boundary: NPC architecture, passive/selective exchange, pore assembly, genome-lamina associations and mechanical phenotypes. The retained evidence is a selected set of primary studies rather than a complete pathway or disease catalogue. Eibauer et al. (2015); Timney et al. (2016); Otsuka et al. (2016); Guelen et al. (2008).

1. Scope: a boundary with several observables

Cryo-electron tomography in Xenopus oocyte envelopes resolved NPC architecture, while separate live-yeast experiments measured passive reporter permeability. Structural snapshots and exchange kinetics are complementary results; one does not by itself prove the mechanism or rate of the other. Eibauer et al. (2015); Timney et al. (2016).

2. Architecture in defined specimens

Eibauer et al. examined the NPC embedded in Xenopus oocyte nuclear envelopes. Turgay et al. resolved a filament meshwork beneath the nuclear periphery in extracted, chromatin-reduced mouse fibroblast preparations. These are specimen-specific structural results, not a universal NPC mass, membrane spacing or live-lamina thickness. Eibauer et al. (2015); Turgay et al. (2017).

3. NPC permeability is gradual for tested reporters

Timney et al. used GFP/Protein-A reporter FRAP in living budding yeast and found a gradual dependence of passive exchange on reporter size. Larger tested reporters could equilibrate more slowly; the study does not support a hard universal molecular-mass cutoff or establish all protein behavior in mammalian cells. Timney et al. (2016).

4. Transport pathways have distinct energy mechanisms

Izaurralde et al. report that disrupting Ran-system asymmetry affected the tested import and export pathways. That is a primary-abstract result, not proof that every nuclear cargo uses the same pathway. Noble et al. separately describe Dbp5 nucleotide regulation by Nup159 and Gle1-IP6, supporting a model of repeated mRNP remodeling cycles. Izaurralde et al. (1997); Noble et al. (2011).

Passive permeability in the Timney yeast experiments and the pathway-specific Ran/Dbp5 findings should remain separate. They do not support describing all nuclear exchange as one energy-dependent electrochemical process. Full methods for the Ran/Dbp5 papers were not retrieved. Timney et al. (2016); Izaurralde et al. (1997); Noble et al. (2011).

5. Remodeling example: interphase pore assembly

Otsuka et al. combined staged human-cell imaging and electron tomography to observe inside-out nuclear-membrane deformation during interphase NPC assembly. The study provides an assembly sequence in the examined cells; it is not a complete account of every mitotic kinase or the full open-mitosis envelope life cycle. Otsuka et al. (2016).

6. Genome associations and mechanical phenotype

In the inspected primary abstract, Guelen et al. report lamina-associated genome domains with low expression in human fibroblasts. Their primary-abstract association map does not establish permanent tethering or repression causality at every locus. Lammerding et al. separately measured increased nuclear deformation and altered selected signaling responses in lamin-A/C-null mouse fibroblasts. Guelen et al. (2008); Lammerding et al. (2004).

The official 2013 Nature corrigendum (DOI 10.1038/nature12316) is linked here: Guelen et al. corrigendum, Nature 500:242. It corrects Fig. 4g and Supplementary Fig. 5f from H3K9me2 to H3K9me3 and revises three Methods data-source statements. The correction notice does not state that the abstract-level lamina-associated-domain findings summarized above change; those findings remain attributed to the inspected primary abstract. Correction text checked 7 October 2026. Full original methods, supplementary data and raw data were not independently appraised.

7. Aging observations retain their biological context

D’Angelo et al. found altered nuclear permeability and pore-component deterioration in aged postmitotic systems, including rat brain nuclei, with supporting worm/culture experiments. This is specific experimental aging evidence, not proof that nuclear-envelope deterioration causes every ordinary human-aging outcome. DAngelo et al. (2009).

8. Conclusions and evidence limits

The checked studies support defined structures, exchange behavior, an interphase assembly sequence, genome associations and mechanical/aging phenotypes. Transferring them between yeast, frog oocytes, prepared or living mammalian cells and human disease requires additional evidence. No exhaustive transport, mitosis or disease validation is claimed. Eibauer et al. (2015); Timney et al. (2016); Otsuka et al. (2016); Lammerding et al. (2004); DAngelo et al. (2009).
