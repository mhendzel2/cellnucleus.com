Overview

Partial evidence review: Banani et al. studied engineered multivalent scaffold systems and reported analogous scaffold-dependent client partitioning in PML nuclear bodies and yeast P bodies. McSwiggen et al. studied HSV-1 replication compartments and reported that accessible viral DNA accounts for much of the observed RNA polymerase II enrichment in that infection context. These two studies illustrate distinct, system-specific mechanisms. They do not establish a general mechanism for all nuclear compartments, an ATP-driven organizing principle for the nucleus, or disease causality.

The eukaryotic cell nucleus is a marvel of spatial and temporal organization. For decades, a central puzzle has been how the cell orchestrates the vast number of molecular components required for complex processes like gene transcription, RNA processing, and DNA replication within the confines of the nucleus, largely in the absence of delimiting lipid membranes. The discovery and characterization of distinct nuclear bodies—such as the nucleolus, Cajal bodies, nuclear speckles, and PML bodies—provided morphological evidence of compartmentalization, but the physical principles governing their assembly and maintenance remained enigmatic.

Study 1

I. Scaffold-Client Partitioning in Cellular Bodies

Banani et al., “Compositional Control of Phase-Separated Cellular Bodies” (Cell, 2016), used engineered multivalent protein and RNA scaffold systems to test client partitioning. In those systems, client recruitment depended on scaffold stoichiometry and client valency. The authors also reported analogous partitioning behavior in mammalian PML nuclear bodies and yeast P bodies.

These findings support composition control in the systems studied. They do not show that every nuclear body forms through the same phase-separation mechanism or that this mechanism explains all features of PML bodies.

Study 2

II. DNA-Mediated Enrichment in HSV-1 Replication Compartments

McSwiggen et al., “Evidence for DNA-mediated nuclear compartmentalization distinct from phase separation” (eLife, 2019), used imaging, single-particle tracking, and perturbations in HSV-1-infected cells. The authors concluded that transient, largely nonspecific interactions with accessible viral DNA account for much of the observed RNA polymerase II enrichment in the replication compartments they studied.

This result is specific to HSV-1 replication compartments and the tested infection conditions. The authors did not establish that every nuclear body uses DNA-mediated enrichment or exclude every possible role for phase separation at other stages of infection.

Evidence limits

  • Primary article: full text at PubMed Central
  • PMID 31038454
  • DOI 10.7554/eLife.47098

Evidence boundary

III. ATP-Wide Organizing Claims Are Not Assessed

Neither checked paper tests ATP hydrolysis as a general, nucleus-wide organizing principle. Banani et al. focus on scaffold interactions, stoichiometry, and client partitioning; McSwiggen et al. focus on molecular behavior in HSV-1 replication compartments.

The broad claim that ATP-dependent processes are the primary design principle for nuclear organization is therefore outside the evidence reviewed on this page.

Evidence limits

  • No general ATP mechanism established
  • No equilibrium-versus-nonequilibrium synthesis attempted

Evidence boundary

IV. Disease Claims Are Not Assessed

The two checked papers do not test whether condensate changes cause cancer, neurodegeneration, or aging. This page makes no disease-mechanism or clinical inference from these studies.

Disease claims require their own disease-specific primary evidence and remain unreviewed in the linked source document.

Evidence limits

  • Cancer claims not appraised
  • Neurodegeneration claims not appraised
  • Aging claims not appraised

Limited synthesis

V. Evidence-Limited Summary and References

The checked papers provide two distinct examples: scaffold-dependent client partitioning in selected cellular bodies, and DNA-mediated RNA polymerase II enrichment in HSV-1 replication compartments. They do not justify a unified mechanism for nuclear organization.
