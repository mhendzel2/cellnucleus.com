Overview

Earlier primary examples: Banani et al. studied engineered multivalent scaffold systems and reported analogous scaffold-dependent client partitioning in PML nuclear bodies and yeast P bodies. McSwiggen et al. studied HSV-1 replication compartments and reported that accessible viral DNA accounts for much of the observed RNA polymerase II enrichment in that infection context. These two studies illustrate distinct, system-specific mechanisms. They do not establish a general mechanism for all nuclear compartments, an ATP-driven organizing principle for the nucleus, or disease causality. Banani et al. (2016); McSwiggen et al. (2019).

Scope: Three Selected Primary Studies

This page is a partial evidence review of three selected primary studies relevant to nuclear compartmentalization. The studies examine different settings and support conclusions limited to those settings.

The original summary made wider claims about nuclear bodies, energy-dependent organization, and disease. Those claims are not supported by the two papers checked here and have been removed from this displayed summary; they remain unreviewed in the linked source document.

Study 1

I. Scaffold-Client Partitioning in Cellular Bodies

Banani et al., “Compositional Control of Phase-Separated Cellular Bodies” (Cell, 2016), used engineered multivalent protein and RNA scaffold systems to test client partitioning. In those systems, client recruitment depended on scaffold stoichiometry and client valency. The authors also reported analogous partitioning behavior in mammalian PML nuclear bodies and yeast P bodies. Banani et al. (2016).

These findings support composition control in the systems studied. They do not show that every nuclear body forms through the same phase-separation mechanism or that this mechanism explains all features of PML bodies. Banani et al. (2016).

Study 2

II. DNA-Mediated Enrichment in HSV-1 Replication Compartments

McSwiggen et al., “Evidence for DNA-mediated nuclear compartmentalization distinct from phase separation” (eLife, 2019), used imaging, single-particle tracking, and perturbations in HSV-1-infected cells. The authors concluded that transient, largely nonspecific interactions with accessible viral DNA account for much of the observed RNA polymerase II enrichment in the replication compartments they studied. McSwiggen et al. (2019).

This result is specific to HSV-1 replication compartments and the tested infection conditions. The authors did not establish that every nuclear body uses DNA-mediated enrichment or exclude every possible role for phase separation at other stages of infection. McSwiggen et al. (2019).

Evidence limits

  • Primary article: original primary article
  • PMID 31038454
  • DOI 10.7554/eLife.47098

Evidence boundary

III. ATP-Wide Organizing Claims Are Not Assessed

The two earlier papers do not test ATP hydrolysis as a general, nucleus-wide organizing principle. Banani et al. focus on scaffold interactions, stoichiometry, and client partitioning; McSwiggen et al. focus on molecular behavior in HSV-1 replication compartments. Banani et al. (2016). McSwiggen et al. (2019).

The broad claim that ATP-dependent processes are the primary design principle for nuclear organization is therefore outside the evidence reviewed on this page.

Evidence limits

  • No general ATP mechanism established
  • No equilibrium-versus-nonequilibrium synthesis attempted

Evidence boundary

IV. Disease Claims Are Not Assessed

The two earlier papers do not test whether condensate changes cause cancer, neurodegeneration, or aging. This page makes no disease-mechanism or clinical inference from these studies. Banani et al. (2016). McSwiggen et al. (2019).

Disease claims require their own disease-specific primary evidence and remain unreviewed in the linked source document.

Limited synthesis

V. Evidence-Limited Summary and References

The two earlier papers provide two distinct examples: scaffold-dependent client partitioning in selected cellular bodies, and DNA-mediated RNA polymerase II enrichment in HSV-1 replication compartments. They do not justify a unified mechanism for nuclear organization.

A third primary example links RNA maturation and compartment structure

Quinodoz and colleagues mapped rRNA maturation by sequencing and imaging and perturbed it in engineered nucleoli. Small-subunit processing defects could reverse layer order and prevent outward rRNA movement. This supplies a nucleolar example in which the RNA substrate helps determine architecture. It complements scaffold-client partitioning and viral-DNA enrichment without making the three mechanisms interchangeable. Quinodoz et al. (2025).
