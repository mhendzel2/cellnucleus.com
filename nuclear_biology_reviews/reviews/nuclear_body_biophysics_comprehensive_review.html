Nuclear Body Biophysics: Coexistence, Networks and Recovery

Primary evidence separates demixing, selective binding, component recovery and polymer organization across defined models.

Biophysics is a comparison of observables

This overview distinguishes molecular binding, composition-dependent coexistence, network connectivity, polymer organization and molecular recovery. These can be related without being interchangeable. The evidence below separates purified mixtures, computational models, Xenopus nucleoli and particular mammalian cellular observations.

Demixing and network formation

Li et al. (2012) observed valency-dependent demixing in purified multivalent protein mixtures and described a likely coupled sol–gel transition. Their qualified interpretation connects collective assembly with network formation; it is not a directly measured universal percolation threshold for nuclear bodies.

Composition and component exchange

Banani et al. (2016) found that client recruitment in model phase-separated scaffolds depends on cognate binding, scaffold composition and valency. Clients and scaffold components also showed different recovery behaviour. Specific molecular interactions and selective exchange therefore can coexist within a phase-separated mixture.

Feric et al. (2016) combined nucleolar observations in Xenopus oocytes with NPM1/fibrillarin/RNA reconstitution. Their two protein-rich condensed phases and surrounding solution had distinct component/material properties. This provides evidence for internal material diversity rather than one viscosity or exchange percentage assigned to an entire nuclear body.

Polymer organization as a separate model

Brackley et al. (2013) simulated multivalent proteins binding polymeric DNA/chromatin. Clustering could arise without an explicitly attractive protein–protein interaction in those models. This supports a possible bridging-driven organization mechanism; it does not prove that a named endogenous body forms by that mechanism.

Erdel et al. (2020) found that the tested mouse-fibroblast chromocenters lacked a separate liquid HP1 pool and interpreted their organization as collapsed polymer globules. The conclusion is specific to that system and does not reject phase separation in other proteins, species or bodies.

Perturbations require specificity

Itoh et al. (2021) observed chromatin motion and compaction changes in human cell models exposed to 1,6-hexanediol. A body's response to that perturbation therefore must be considered alongside effects on chromatin. This is an inference about assay specificity rather than a universal rejection of LLPS.

Evidence assessment

The checked studies support context-specific coexistence, selective sorting, distinct component dynamics and polymer-associated organization. These observations motivate comparing composition, material properties and cellular context together. They do not justify universal viscosity numbers, categorical exchange curves or a single phase label for all nuclear bodies.

Evidence context and limits

Li et al: Engineered multivalent SH3 and proline-rich-motif proteins and PTB/RNA reconstitution; valency-dependent phase diagrams; droplet/bulk partitioning; dynamic light scattering, SAXS, photobleaching and cryo-EM; additional HeLa puncta and nephrin/Nck/N-WASP reconstitution context. Descriptive primary observations/modelling at the recorded source depth; no operational procedures reproduced. Sol–gel coupling is the authors’ qualified interpretation, not a directly measured universal percolation threshold or nuclear material state. The page preserves likely and confines claims to reconstituted systems.

Banani et al: Purified engineered polySUMO/polySIM, polySH3/polyPRM, and PTB/polyUCUCU scaffold pairs with fluorescent low-valency clients; phase diagrams, partition coefficients, binding mutants, valency series, and mass-action modeling. Current page claims stay with these engineered systems. Descriptive primary observations/modelling at the recorded source depth; no operational procedures reproduced. SUMO/SIM mutant/GFP controls are not described as controls for every scaffold pair. Does not show all endogenous bodies demix or assign a phase mechanism solely from enrichment.

Feric et al: Amplified extrachromosomal nucleoli in X. laevis oocytes, fluorescent subcompartment markers, actin perturbation to permit fusion; purified NPM1/FIB1/rRNA mixtures; FRAP and embedded 50-nm probe microrheology; ATP depletion in oocyte and mammalian-cell conditions. Descriptive primary observations/modelling at the recorded source depth; no operational procedures reproduced. Two protein-rich phases are not a reconstitution of all three nucleolar compartments or a universal nuclear-body model. Aging and ATP observations are component/condition-specific; no standalone FRAP-to-solid-state inference.

Brackley et al: LAMMPS Brownian-dynamics bead-and-spring DNA/chromatin polymers with reversibly binding model proteins. Protein–protein and template–template interactions have steric repulsion only. Bridging, monovalent controls, protein-size variation, and chromatin simulations informed by Pol II/NFkB ChIP-seq; comparisons with experimental contact data. Descriptive primary observations/modelling at the recorded source depth; no operational procedures reproduced. Finite time, chosen coarse-graining/interactions, and starting conformations limit in-cell inference. Chromosome territory behavior depends on initial conditions and some simulated cluster organization differs from cells. This is simulation evidence, not diagnosis of a named endogenous focus.

Erdel et al: Mouse-fibroblast pericentric chromocenters; descriptive fluorescence mobility and compartment/compaction observations only. Descriptive primary observations/modelling at the recorded source depth; no operational procedures reproduced. This check does not reappraise detailed methods or raw data. The conclusion is confined to the tested mouse chromocenters and is not a universal binding-site mechanism or rejection of LLPS.

Itoh et al: Human cell-line chromatin assessed through fluorescent histone markers. This is not a neuronal FUS study. Single-nucleosome motion measurements and chromatin imaging. Chromatin motion/compaction effects in human cell models; no neuronal FUS inference or mechanism diagnosis of every condensate.
