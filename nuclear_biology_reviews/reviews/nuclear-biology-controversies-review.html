Reading the evidence

The examples below distinguish a structural observation, an interpretation of its physical mechanism, and a claim about biological function. These are separate questions: imaging can establish an arrangement without uniquely identifying its mechanism, and a material-state observation does not by itself establish a functional cause. This is an editorial framework for comparing the cited studies.

Chromatin: fibres and single-cell domains

Nishino et al. (2012) examined human mitotic HeLa chromosomes using cryo-electron microscopy and X-ray scattering. Their results support predominantly irregularly folded nucleosome fibres rather than regular 30-nm chromatin organization in that sample. This challenges a universal regular-fibre account; it does not show that ordered fibres are absent from every tissue, cell-cycle state or species.

Bintu et al. (2018) found TAD-like domains in individual human cell models, with boundary positions varying between cells and preferential positions associated with CTCF/cohesin sites. After cohesin loss, population-average TADs disappeared while individual TAD-like domains persisted without the same preferred boundary positions. A population boundary therefore should not be treated as a fixed boundary in every cell.

Condensates: evidence depends on the system

Feric et al. (2016) combined observations of Xenopus oocyte nucleoli with reconstitution of NPM1-rich and fibrillarin-rich condensed phases in an RNA-containing mixture. The reconstituted system contains two protein-rich phases plus the surrounding solution, with different component dynamics. This is evidence for those nucleolar components and models, rather than a demonstration that every nuclear compartment is a uniform liquid.

Phase separation can include selective molecular interactions. In the model droplets of Banani et al. (2016), client recruitment depended on cognate binding sites, scaffold composition and valency. Selective enrichment therefore does not refute LLPS; enrichment alone also does not prove that an endogenous compartment demixes.

Erdel et al. (2020) found that the pericentric heterochromatin studied in mouse fibroblasts lacked a separate liquid HP1 pool. They interpreted those chromocenters as collapsed polymer globules rather than HP1-driven liquid droplets. This is a system-specific counterexample to a universal LLPS account, not a rejection of phase separation in nucleoli or every form of heterochromatin.

The coexistence and component-recovery observations in Feric et al. (2016) and Banani et al. (2016) address different properties. Fluorescence recovery describes molecular recovery; composition and coexistence address demixing. This overview therefore treats recovery and appearance as supporting observations whose interpretation depends on the other evidence in each study.

The nuclear periphery: a bounded mechanics example

Lammerding et al. (2004) reported increased nuclear deformation and impaired mechanically associated transcriptional responses in lamin A/C-deficient mouse embryonic fibroblasts. The study connects nuclear mechanics and transcriptional responses in that model. It does not resolve how every distinct human lamin mutation produces a tissue-specific phenotype.

What these examples establish

The checked examples support irregular mitotic chromosome organization in one human sample, variable single-cell domain boundaries, selective sorting within model phases, context-dependent condensate mechanisms, and a connection between lamin deficiency and nuclear mechanics. None establishes a single mechanism governing all nuclear organization. Claims about universal architecture or functional causation need evidence matched to the specific biological system.
