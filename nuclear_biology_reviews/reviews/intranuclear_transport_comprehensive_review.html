Abstract

This concise review examines molecular mobility within nuclei through inert-probe FCS, binding-aware FRAP models, single-particle tracking and selected nucleolar reconstitution experiments. It separates measured motion from interpretations of crowding or material state, and identifies the relevant probe, cell system and fitting assumptions. Transport across the nuclear envelope is a separate observable. Dross et al. (2009); Sprague et al. (2004); Hansen et al. (2018); Feric et al. (2016); Timney et al. (2016).

Interpretation Principles

Probe size and shape. eGFP oligomers showed size-dependent nuclear mobility; comparisons in solution also examined their elongated shape. Dross et al. (2009).

Binding models. Recovery curves can reflect diffusion and binding; not every curve independently identifies every parameter. Sprague et al. (2004).

Chromatin accessibility. The tested eGFP oligomers accessed dense chromatin. This result does not predict behavior of every chromatin-binding protein. Dross et al. (2009).

Material state. NPM1 and fibrillarin droplets showed different behavior under the tested conditions. A whole nucleus should not be assigned one of those measured states. Feric et al. (2016).

What Nuclear Mobility Measurements Support

Dross and colleagues combined calibrated FCS with H2A-mRFP chromatin imaging in living HEK293, HeLa, TP366 and T98G cells. They measured eGFP mono- through tetramers outside nucleoli, fitted two diffusing populations with a normal-diffusion model, and found both size-dependent mobility and access to dense chromatin. Local fitted mobility did not correlate with the chromatin-label intensity at their measurement scale. Dross et al. (2009).

The linked oligomers were also studied in solution and compared with shape-dependent calculations. The results therefore do not uniquely attribute slower mobility to chromatin sieving, nor establish one universal diffusion coefficient, nuclear-to-water slowdown or subdiffusion exponent. Dross et al. (2009).

Fluorescence Methods and Their Limits

FCS infers mobility from fluorescence-fluctuation correlations within a calibrated observation volume. Dross and colleagues fitted two diffusing components and a nonfluorescent contribution using a normal-diffusion model. They checked reference-dye calibration, temperature, cell movement and oligomer integrity; these controls are part of interpreting the reported coefficients. Dross et al. (2009).

FRAP recovery can combine diffusion with binding reactions. Sprague and colleagues derived reaction-diffusion solutions for single or multiple independent binding interactions and described constraints on what can be estimated from a recovery curve. This methodological statement follows the primary abstract, without claiming a full appraisal of its experimental protocol. Sprague et al. (2004).

For single-particle tracking, Hansen and colleagues used simulated data and live-cell experiments to test Spot-On population inference. Motion blur can undercount fast particles, and loss out of the focal plane can bias inferred fractions. Their displacement model accounts for localization error and defocalization, but assumes Brownian populations and can be inaccurate for frequent state transitions at the imaging timescale. Hansen et al. (2018).

Compare Experimental Contexts

Pore equilibration, local FCS correlation times, FRAP recovery and particle-displacement distributions quantify different aspects of mobility. Their comparison requires the reporter, cell system, measurement region, acquisition interval and fitted model. An FCS diffusion coefficient should not be relabeled as a whole-compartment exchange rate. Timney et al. (2016); Dross et al. (2009); Sprague et al. (2004); Hansen et al. (2018).

The primary examples here support heterogeneity within a probe population and differences between tested nucleolar components. They do not establish a universal kinetic signature for each nuclear body, or that every such signature is optimally tuned to biological function. The related source draft has additional comparisons that have not received this systematic review. Dross et al. (2009); Feric et al. (2016); Hansen et al. (2018).

Selected Nucleolar Material Experiments

In Feric's experiments, purified NPM1 droplets had comparatively liquid-like probe motion, whereas fibrillarin droplets showed subdiffusive probe motion and slower relaxation. Fibrillarin photobleaching recovery declined as the in-vitro droplets aged; NPM1 droplets retained substantially greater recovery. These are component- and preparation-specific observations. Feric et al. (2016).

The study proposed that layered material properties could influence sequential ribosomal-RNA processing. That functional proposal is distinct from directly demonstrating an increased ribosome-assembly rate caused by phase separation. This summary does not assign unverified diffusion coefficients, surface tensions or exchange rates to nuclear speckles, Cajal bodies or PML bodies. Feric et al. (2016).

Mathematical Interpretation and Assumptions

A normal Brownian model has mean-squared displacement proportional to elapsed time. Dross used normal-diffusion FCS fits; Hansen used Brownian displacement populations while separately testing sensitivity to anomalous behavior. Model choice must therefore be distinguished from a finding that all nuclear motion is Brownian. Dross et al. (2009); Hansen et al. (2018).

A power-law fit, MSD(t) proportional to talpha, labels sublinear motion when alpha is below one; Feric reported this behavior for tracer motion in reconstituted fibrillarin droplets. That result is not an exponent for all nucleoplasmic molecules. Feric et al. (2016).

The earlier single effective-diffusion equation has been withdrawn here because its rapid-exchange and immobile-bound-state assumptions were not tied to a checked application. Reaction-diffusion FRAP models and multi-population SPT fits provide explicit alternatives; their parameters remain conditional on their models and measurements. Sprague et al. (2004); Hansen et al. (2018).

Conclusions and Experimental Questions

The strongest conclusions retained here identify their reporter and model: size-dependent passive passage in yeast, chromatin-accessible eGFP probes in human nuclei, acquisition-sensitive SPT population estimates and distinct behavior of selected nucleolar proteins. Extending them to another cargo or cell state requires measurements in that context. Timney et al. (2016); Dross et al. (2009); Hansen et al. (2018); Feric et al. (2016).

Useful next questions include whether the same probe behaves similarly in another cell state, whether an inferred binding population is robust to imaging settings, and whether perturbing nucleolar material organization changes RNA-processing outcomes. These are proposed tests, not reported answers or clinical applications. Dross et al. (2009); Hansen et al. (2018); Feric et al. (2016).
