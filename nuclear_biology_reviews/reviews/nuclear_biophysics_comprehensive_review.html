Scope: several physical questions

Nuclear biophysics includes different experimental observables. Intact-cell strain experiments assess nuclear deformation, isolated-nucleus pulling assesses force-extension response, and fluorescent tracers assess molecular mobility. These studies do not measure one interchangeable material constant. Lammerding et al. (2004); Stephens et al. (2017); Dross et al. (2009).

Selected findings and their contexts

Lamin-A/C-null mouse fibroblasts deformed more under applied cell strain than wild-type controls. In a separate isolated-nucleus assay, chromatin contributed strongly to the short-extension response, whereas lamin A/C primarily influenced the larger-extension response. These are context-specific mechanical results, not a ranked stiffness catalogue of human tissues. Lammerding et al. (2004); Stephens et al. (2017).

FCS measurements found that eGFP oligomer mobility varied spatially in several human cell lines without correlating with the local H2A-mRFP chromatin-intensity proxy at the resolution used. This tracer result does not measure whole-nucleus stiffness. Dross et al. (2009).

Structural evidence and mechanical inference

Cryo-electron tomography of extracted, chromatin-reduced mouse fibroblast nuclei resolved a lamin filament meshwork. That structural evidence establishes organization in the examined preparations; it does not by itself measure live-cell elasticity. Turgay et al. (2017).

The shell-and-interior model in the isolated-nucleus pulling study helps explain the observed deformation regimes. Chromatin compaction and lamin-A/C perturbations support separable mechanical contributions in that assay; the model should not be treated as a universal description of all nuclear material. Stephens et al. (2017).

Compare quantities before comparing values

Stephens et al. report force-extension slopes as nuclear spring constants. Their short versus long extension regimes, with a crossover near 3 micrometres in the tested assay, should not be converted into a universal Young modulus without an additional geometry/material model. Stephens et al. (2017).

Dross et al. infer diffusion coefficients from FCS using calibrated observation volumes and a two-component normal-diffusion fit. Probe shape, calibration and the fitted populations affect interpretation; a fluorescent-protein diffusion coefficient is not directly a whole-nucleus viscosity measurement. Dross et al. (2009).

Plots require traceable experimental data

This concise page presents qualitative findings with their primary study contexts. The earlier cross-cell stiffness and disease-comparison plots lacked a traceable evidence chain and were withdrawn. The checked pulling, tracer and cell-strain studies remain separate rather than being combined into a numerical ranking. Stephens et al. (2017); Dross et al. (2009); Lammerding et al. (2004).

What the experiments measure

In the intact-cell study, nuclear deformation was normalized to applied membrane strain. Magnetic beads on the cell surface supplied cytoskeletal mechanics controls; those bead results are not intranuclear microrheology. The authors found both altered nuclear mechanics and a softer cytoskeletal response in lamin-A/C-null fibroblasts. Lammerding et al. (2004).

The isolated-nucleus study measured deformation under pulling and compared chromatin/lamin perturbations. The FCS study examined fluorescent oligomers outside nucleoli. Isolation, probe selection and spatial resolution define the questions each experiment can answer. Stephens et al. (2017); Dross et al. (2009).

Disease relevance without a universal phenotype

Lamin-A/C-null mouse fibroblasts had lower viability under mechanical strain and altered expression of selected strain-responsive genes. This loss-of-function model is evidence for a biological connection between lamins, mechanics and signaling; it does not establish the same mechanism for every human laminopathy. Lammerding et al. (2004).

Confined-migration imaging observed transient nuclear-envelope rupture and repair in selected cancer-cell and fibroblast models. These observations do not establish a general clinical stiffness biomarker or that all cancers have softer nuclei. Denais et al. (2016).

Biochemical responses remain specific

In the lamin-A/C-null fibroblast study, mechanically induced egr-1 and iex-1 expression was reduced, while other tested responses remained intact. NF-kB-dependent transcription was attenuated despite increased binding in the tested stimulation context. The result concerns selected pathways rather than nonspecific failure of all transcription. Lammerding et al. (2004).

Protein/RNA droplet experiments and Xenopus oocyte imaging found distinct behavior of NPM1-rich and FIB1-rich nucleolar components. Those findings provide a specific example of compartment-dependent material behavior, not a measured viscosity for the entire mammalian nucleus. Feric et al. (2016).

Interpretation limits and open questions

A comparison should retain cell/preparation identity, probe, measured quantity, deformation range and model assumptions. The checked studies support different questions about local mobility, compartment behavior and nuclear deformation; transferring their parameters to another cell state requires additional evidence. Stephens et al. (2017); Dross et al. (2009); Feric et al. (2016).

Mechanical defects and altered signaling coexist in the lamin-A/C-null model, but the study does not establish a single causal route for all tissue-specific human disease. No diagnostic accuracy or therapeutic effectiveness is inferred here. Lammerding et al. (2004).
