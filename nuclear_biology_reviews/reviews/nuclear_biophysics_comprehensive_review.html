Nuclear Biophysics: A Decade of Composite Mechanics and Molecular Organization

Integrated structural, transport and force-based evidence through 2026

Download the matching Word review

Abstract

Nuclear biophysics connects the organization of chromatin and nuclear proteins with force transmission, molecular transport and material response. Over the past decade, primary experiments have resolved how chromatin and lamins contribute differently to deformation, how nuclear-body composition creates local material environments, and how envelope integrity can fail and recover. More recent work adds direct local force application, metabolically active nuclear preparations and quantitative coupling between density, volume and transport. The resulting nucleus is a structured, active composite whose physical properties depend on spatial scale, time scale and biological state. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5] [6]

This review integrates those developments without treating them as measurements of one universal nuclear stiffness or viscosity. It distinguishes structure from mechanical function, transport from binding, and material response from downstream regulation. Current 2024–2026 studies strengthen connections between chromatin tethering, nuclear-body organization and pore plasticity, while leaving important questions about causality and physiological generality open. [7] [8] [9] [10] [11]

1. The nucleus as a coupled physical system

A useful physical description separates the chromatin network, accessible nucleoplasmic spaces, concentrated nuclear bodies and the envelope-associated boundary, then asks how they interact. Each component supplies a different observable. Inert proteins report movement through accessible space; a labeled locus remains embedded in chromatin; a nuclear-body fusion event probes collective relaxation; stretching the nuclear boundary loads a composite structure. These differences explain why nuclear measurements can disagree numerically without contradicting one another. The measurements must first be matched by component, geometry and time scale before a common material interpretation is attempted. [12] [13] [2] [1]

The word composite also implies coupling. A force delivered to the envelope does not necessarily load all chromatin equally. A molecule concentrated in a nucleolus does not necessarily share the environment of a nearby soluble molecule. The mechanical and chemical connections between these components can change during perturbation. Attar and colleagues modeled peripheral chromatin links as a route for load transmission, while Zhao and colleagues observed regional condensate responses during nuclear deformation. The nucleus should therefore be described by component properties together with their connections, rather than by an average value that hides those connections. [8] [10] [2]

2. Structure identifies candidates, not mechanical constants

Cryo-electron structural analysis by Turgay and colleagues resolved lamin filament organization in a prepared somatic-cell system. Such work establishes molecular architecture at the lamina, whereas its mechanical contribution requires a separate perturbation or force experiment. Likewise, modern nuclear-pore structures distinguish scaffold, linker and cytoplasmic-face components but do not alone measure transport rates or stress response. Structural models narrow the plausible mechanisms and identify interfaces to test. They should not be used to infer a material modulus solely from an image or to assign a universal pore diameter to all cell types and mechanical states. [14] [15] [16] [11]

Genome-position measurements supply another structural level. Guelen and colleagues mapped broad lamina-associated domains in human fibroblasts, linking peripheral association with a characteristic genomic organization. These maps are evidence of association, not direct measurements of tether strength or permanent attachment in every cell. The associated corrigendum corrects the identity and provenance of a chromatin-mark dataset and should accompany the original reference. Recent tether-depletion studies make the distinction productive by testing which envelope proteins are necessary for positioning and what changes after short versus persistent loss. [17] [18] [9]

3. Chromatin and lamins contribute in different regimes

Stephens and colleagues used isolated-nucleus deformation to distinguish response regimes: chromatin contributed strongly at smaller extensions, whereas lamin A supported strain stiffening at larger extensions. This connects biochemical state to mechanical resistance without making one component the sole determinant of nuclear mechanics. The measured force–extension behavior depends on geometry and loading conditions. A slope in this assay is not directly interchangeable with an indentation modulus or a local viscosity. The useful mechanistic conclusion concerns how the relative load-bearing contributions change with deformation. [1]

Bergamaschi and colleagues extended the picture with optical-tweezer measurements accompanied by images of chromatin and the nuclear boundary, interpreting heterogeneous response through a hierarchical chain model. Attar and colleagues examined how peripheral tethering allows chromatin structure to affect force resistance in a polymer model constrained by experiments. These developments emphasize that internal organization and boundary coupling must be considered together. A chromatin change can alter global mechanics only to the extent that the imposed load engages that changed structure, and the form of engagement is itself an experimental variable. [7] [8]

4. Molecular mobility is another scale of physical organization

The eGFP-oligomer experiments of Dross and colleagues measured size-dependent mobility with calibrated FCS in living nuclear regions outside nucleoli. Erdel and colleagues combined tracer-size variation with modeling to identify intermediate-scale behavior consistent with obstacles and polymer organization. These observations show how the physical environment experienced by molecules depends on their dimensions and the scale of observation. They do not imply that every DNA-binding factor has the same mobility, because specific association with chromatin adds another process. Molecular search is consequently influenced by both the accessible environment and the binding chemistry of the searching protein. [12] [13] [19]

FRAP and single-particle tracking help resolve that distinction when their limitations are made explicit. Reaction–diffusion FRAP analysis separates transport and binding contributions only in identifiable model regimes. Spot-On demonstrated how blur and focal-volume escape bias apparent populations in tracking experiments. Such corrections matter when a mechanobiological perturbation changes the mobile fraction. A slower protein should not automatically be described as evidence of nuclear stiffening or increased viscosity; conversely, a mechanical softening need not accelerate a tightly bound factor. The two observations can be connected only by testing the relevant coupling. [20] [21] [19]

5. Nuclear bodies combine composition and material response

Feric and colleagues demonstrated coexisting nucleolar subcompartments and component-specific dynamics in living systems and reconstituted droplets. The in-vitro tracer-viscosity experiment and the in-vivo fusion experiment measured different quantities, including viscosity and viscosity relative to surface tension. This supports a physically organized nucleolus while retaining preparation and component specificity. A spherical shape, exchange of fluorescent molecules or fusion event alone should not substitute for a complete material characterization. Different molecular mechanisms can produce some similar imaging behaviors, and the surrounding chromatin can also constrain collective motion. [2] [22] [10]

The 2024 confined-migration work of Zhao, Xia and Brangwynne makes that surrounding environment explicit. Chromatin became regionally heterogeneous as nuclei passed through microfluidic constrictions, with different opportunities for condensate formation in advancing and trailing regions. Endogenous-body deformation and model-assembly behavior were both studied, supplying complementary observations. The result connects nuclear mechanics with compartment organization without requiring a uniform global crowding change or a universal phase-separation mechanism. Local protein concentration and mechanical permissiveness jointly define the relevant physical conditions. [10]

6. Active remodeling and persistent responses

Rashid and colleagues directly loaded chromatin with approximately 200-nm-diameter targeted magnetic particles and connected local deformation with persistent changes in mobility and regulatory readouts. Their CHO chromatin-reporter and induced-pluripotent-stem-cell polymerase experiments are distinct biological arms. The lasting response after force cessation is not identical to the immediate viscoelastic phase response during oscillatory loading. It supplies a mechanism-oriented example of nuclear mechanomemory whose generality must be tested in other cell types and genomic contexts. [4]

Byfield and colleagues used metabolically active karyoplasts to probe dissipation and large deformation, with ATP-production and BRM/BRG1-remodeling perturbations. The work argues that active internal processes can permit nuclear fluidization and shape change. It also shows why loading hysteresis should not be assigned exclusively to passive viscosity. The preparation preserves a membrane-enclosed active nuclear system; it differs from detergent-isolated nuclei and from a nucleus still coupled to the full cell. Such preparation differences are biological differences in the system, rather than nuisance details to remove from the discussion. [5] [23]

7. Envelope coupling, transport and failure

The SUN–nesprin experiments of Crisp and colleagues established a molecular route linking inner and outer nuclear-membrane proteins across the perinuclear space, supporting the LINC-complex model. This gives external force a plausible connection to nuclear structure, while specific mechanical and transport experiments are needed to establish its functional consequences. Elosegui-Artola and colleagues showed force-dependent YAP nuclear entry and changes in import behavior in defined cell systems. The result demonstrates a regulated cargo response rather than a universal rule that every protein crosses more rapidly whenever the nucleus flattens. [24] [25]

Envelope rupture presents a different physical and biological state. Denais and colleagues observed loss of compartmentalization during constrained migration and studied ESCRT-III-dependent resealing alongside damage-associated responses. This does not mean every deformed nucleus ruptures, or that restoration of a localization reporter proves all DNA lesions have been repaired. The mechanical challenge and the subsequent recovery involve membrane integrity, molecular redistribution and genome consequences that need distinct readouts. A current account of nuclear function should preserve those stages rather than collapsing them into a binary healthy-versus-damaged shape classification. [3] [26]

8. Current advances connect density, pores and genome tethers

The density-homeostasis study of Biswas and colleagues connects nuclear volume to imported macromolecules and chromatin through a pressure-balance model supported by measurements in several systems. Hoffmann and colleagues studied pressure-dependent nucleocytosolic flow in Dictyostelium and related it to pore dilation. These are complementary physical questions: one concerns volume and concentration, the other flow under a pressure difference. Neither is identical to passive protein exchange or receptor-mediated cargo transport. Together they broaden nuclear biophysics beyond structural stiffness to include composition, osmotic balance and membrane-crossing mechanics. [6] [27]

Pore plasticity and genome positioning are also becoming more experimentally resolved. Morgan and colleagues used pan-expansion microscopy to examine NPC diameter distributions, surface biases and perturbations. Lewis and colleagues identified redundant contributions from LBR and membrane-bound LAP2 isoforms to peripheral heterochromatin positioning in mammalian cells. These studies show that neither pore geometry nor chromatin attachment should be regarded as a fixed universal boundary condition. Understanding their dynamic regulation will require linking structural measurements to matched transport, force and transcription assays in the same biological context. [11] [9] [8]

A recent biological example comes from McCreery and colleagues’ study of human embryo and primed-pluripotent-cell models. Growth-factor state was linked to nuclear deformation, osmotic stress and chromatin regulation during fate transitions. Mechanical or osmotic priming could accelerate transitions, while sustained biochemical signals remained necessary for robust lineage induction. This connects nuclear physical organization to a regulated developmental context and shows why a mechanical response should be interpreted together with the signaling state in which it occurs. [29]

An integrated account also needs to distinguish reversible adaptation from loss of compartment integrity. Regional condensate changes during confinement can accompany deformation without being identical to envelope rupture, while the restoration of a leakage reporter after rupture does not establish that chromatin organization is fully restored. Following both body organization and boundary integrity can identify which response comes first and whether it persists after migration. This temporal information helps connect the immediate mechanical event to subsequent nuclear function rather than treating every endpoint image as the same biological state. [10] [3]

The link between density and active response remains especially promising. A transport-driven change in imported macromolecules can alter the pressure balance and nuclear volume, while remodeling activity can change how chromatin accommodates load. These mechanisms could interact during a state transition, but their relationship is not established simply because both respond to substrate or growth-factor conditions. A joint experiment should track composition, nuclear geometry, loading response and regulatory state. Such an approach can test whether changes in nuclear volume are a cause, consequence or partner of changes in mechanical behavior. [6] [5] [4]

The most informative comparisons preserve both the observable and the state of the cell. A nuclear mechanics phenotype can coexist with an unchanged soluble-probe diffusivity, and a pore diameter change can coexist with cargo-specific regulation. Matched studies should report location, scale, loading regime, specimen preparation and the exact molecular reporter. A disagreement then becomes a concrete question about component recruitment or regulation. Without that information, a collection of numerical stiffness or viscosity values can create apparent precision while comparing physically different systems. [23] [1] [12] [11] [25]

10. Synthesis and research priorities

The past decade established that nuclear structure, transport and mechanics are coupled while retaining distinct physical descriptions. Current work is beginning to identify the connections: peripheral tethering recruits chromatin into force response, active remodeling contributes dissipation, spatial heterogeneity affects condensates, and transport helps set nuclear density. The next step is joint causal measurement across these levels. Tracking the sequence of structural, mechanical and regulatory changes in one biological transition will be more revealing than assigning a universal nuclear material label or treating a single assay as sufficient for all mechanisms.
