Overview

A viscosity estimate must identify the material and the model used. Molecular diffusion in living nuclei, probe motion in purified nucleolar-protein droplets and whole-nucleus force-extension responses are different measurements. The selected results below are retained in their own experimental contexts. Dross et al. (2009); Feric et al. (2016); Stephens et al. (2017).

1. Viscosity is a model-specific inference

Feric et al. inferred a viscosity for reconstituted NPM1 droplets when their embedded probes moved approximately diffusively. FIB1 droplets instead showed subdiffusive motion, inconsistent with the same simple-viscous-liquid description. This distinction concerns particular protein/RNA droplets rather than one material constant for the entire nucleus. Feric et al. (2016).

2. Different assays return different quantities

FCS in the Dross study returned fitted fluorescent-oligomer diffusion coefficients. Particle tracking in the Feric study measured displacements within reconstituted droplets. Stephens et al. measured force-extension response of isolated nuclei. Diffusion coefficients, inferred droplet viscosity and nuclear spring constants are not interchangeable units or observables. Dross et al. (2009); Feric et al. (2016); Stephens et al. (2017).

3. Nucleoplasmic tracer mobility

Dross et al. measured eGFP monomers through tetramers in several human cell lines outside nucleoli, fitting normal diffusion with two populations. They found spatial mobility variation without a correlation to local chromatin-intensity proxy at their resolution. These measurements do not establish a homogeneous nucleoplasm or a universal viscosity for all proteins. Dross et al. (2009).

Reporter-size dependence differed from a simple globular-protein expectation in both solution and nuclei. Probe shape and interactions therefore remain relevant when deciding whether mobility can be interpreted as a fluid-property measurement. Dross et al. (2009).

4. Chromatin mechanics is a separate observable

In isolated-nucleus pulling, chromatin compaction/digestion changed primarily the short-extension response. The authors interpreted chromatin as an important mechanical interior in that geometry. Their force-extension measurements did not provide a universal chromatin shear viscosity or establish one gel/melt state for all living nuclei. Stephens et al. (2017).

5. A traceable droplet viscosity example

For purified NPM1 droplets in Feric et al., probes of radius 50 nanometres gave a motion exponent of 0.92 ± 0.06 and a reported inferred viscosity of 0.74 ± 0.06 Pa·s. These are the paper’s droplet-specific values, conditional on the approximately simple-liquid interpretation; they are not measurements of the whole nucleolus or mammalian nucleus. Feric et al. (2016).

FIB1 droplet probes had a reported subdiffusive exponent of 0.5 ± 0.1. FIB1 recovery also declined as the reconstituted droplets aged, whereas NPM1 recovery remained nearly complete over the tested period. Subdiffusive/aging material should not be summarized by the NPM1 simple-liquid viscosity. Feric et al. (2016).

6. Lamins and deformation-dependent stiffness

Lamin-A/C depletion mainly altered the large-extension response in the Stephens isolated-nucleus assay, with its roughly 3-micrometre crossover. That result supports a deformation-dependent contribution from the lamina in this preparation; it is not a viscosity measurement of an elastic boundary. Stephens et al. (2017).

7. Compare like quantities in like systems

The evidence spans small fluorescent proteins, embedded particles in purified droplets and mechanically pulled nuclei. Differences among these results cannot by themselves be attributed to a universal scale-dependent viscosity law: probe, preparation, molecular interactions, fit assumptions and measured quantity all change. Dross et al. (2009); Feric et al. (2016); Stephens et al. (2017).

Hansen et al. explicitly modeled single-particle displacement distributions with Brownian subpopulations and corrected localization/defocalization effects. Such assumptions must be assessed before a mobility result is assigned a material interpretation. Hansen et al. (2018).

8. Regulation and functional interpretation

Feric et al. observed distinct NPM1/FIB1 recovery behavior in Xenopus oocyte nucleoli and after ATP depletion. Their proposed link between layered material properties and ribosomal processing is a mechanistic interpretation, not a direct proof that a measured viscosity controls all transcription or disease. Feric et al. (2016).

The retained evidence supports specific differences between compartments and assay conditions. Additional matched measurements are needed before transferring a droplet viscosity or protein-mobility result to another nuclear material, cell type or biological state. Feric et al. (2016); Dross et al. (2009).
