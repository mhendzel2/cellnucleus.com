Overview

Local probe-motion measurements require a defined probe, compartment and physical model. This concise review separates passive microrheology in reconstituted nucleolar-protein droplets, fluorescent-protein mobility analysis, and force-extension measurements of nuclei. None is a universal nuclear rheology spectrum. Feric et al. (2016); Hansen et al. (2018); Stephens et al. (2017).

Part I. Observables, models and acquisition limits

Feric et al. tracked fluctuating embedded particles in reconstituted NPM1 and FIB1 droplets. Approximately diffusive motion supported a simple-liquid interpretation for the measured NPM1 droplets; subdiffusive FIB1 motion instead supported viscoelastic behavior. The material inference depends on the observed motion and chosen model. Feric et al. (2016).

Protein tracking addresses a related but different question. Hansen et al. fitted displacement distributions of free/bound Brownian subpopulations under steady-state assumptions without state transitions in the core model, while correcting localization error and loss of fast molecules from focus. A fitted bound fraction is not automatically a rheological modulus. Hansen et al. (2018).

Dross et al. calibrated FCS observation volumes and used a two-population normal-diffusion fit for eGFP oligomers. Acquisition conditions and probe geometry affected interpretation. Slow fluctuations can reflect more than a single homogeneous fluid, so a reporter-mobility fit needs its own checks. Dross et al. (2009).

These papers provide descriptive assay comparisons. This page does not supply experimental protocols, a universal conversion of every displacement curve to a complex modulus, or evidence that all nuclear fluctuations satisfy one equilibrium material model. Feric et al. (2016); Hansen et al. (2018); Dross et al. (2009).

Part II. Local material behavior versus nuclear deformation

Reconstituted NPM1/FIB1 droplets differed in probe motion, fusion and recovery. FIB1 recovery decreased as droplets aged, while NPM1 remained more recoverable over the tested period. Their composition- and time-dependent responses are a specific material comparison, not a generic statement about all nuclear condensates. Feric et al. (2016).

The eGFP oligomers studied by Dross et al. accessed dense chromatin regions, and measured mobility did not correlate with the H2A-mRFP intensity proxy at the sampled resolution. Nucleoli were excluded from those FCS points. This result does not establish the material properties of nucleoli or of larger chromatin-bound assemblies. Dross et al. (2009).

Stephens et al. pulled isolated nuclei and resolved a short-extension regime strongly affected by chromatin and a larger-extension regime primarily affected by lamin A/C. The approximately 3-micrometre crossover is a reported property of that deformation assay, not a particle size defining a universal nuclear mesh. Stephens et al. (2017).

Lammerding et al. combined intact-cell strain/nuclear deformation imaging with beads attached to the cell surface. Their magnetic-bead controls probed cytoskeletal mechanics and must not be relabeled as embedded-particle nuclear viscosity. Lammerding et al. (2004).

Part III. Evidence standards for comparison

Hansen et al. used simulated ground truth and experiments with defined fluorescent reporters to assess bias from localization, defocalization, motion blur and model assumptions. The paper’s tested inference framework provides a way to distinguish acquisition bias from a change in molecular populations; it is not validation of every later tracking experiment. Hansen et al. (2018).

Dross and Feric studied different compartments and probes, and Stephens measured nucleus-scale deformation. A comparison should therefore state compartment, preparation, probe interactions, time window, observable and fit assumptions before treating a difference as a material-property change. Dross et al. (2009); Feric et al. (2016); Stephens et al. (2017).

The checked studies support local, context-specific material and mobility measurements. A link from such measurements to genome regulation or disease requires separate biological evidence; none of these assays alone establishes a universal causal mechanism. Feric et al. (2016); Hansen et al. (2018); Stephens et al. (2017).
