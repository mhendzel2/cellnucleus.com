Nuclear Transport & Dynamics

Nuclear Pore Complex: Structure, Function, and Regulation of Nuclear Transport

Published: June 2024
Scientific text reviewed: October 3, 2026
Concise evidence review
Dr. Michael Hendzel, University of Alberta

Nuclear Pore Complex Structure

Topic illustration using an icon and color gradient; no published structural image is reproduced here. For experimental structural data, see Eibauer et al. (2015), Structure and gating of the nuclear pore complex.

Abstract

Nuclear pore complexes (NPCs) connect the nucleus and cytoplasm through the nuclear envelope. Structural studies resolve scaffold rings and asymmetric components, while transport assays show a size-dependent permeability barrier whose behavior depends on the experimental system. This concise review separates structural evidence, passive permeability, selected transport and assembly mechanisms, and specific animal disease or aging models. Bley et al. (2022); Timney et al. (2016).

Key Points

  • Human and fungal structural work supports an eight-spoke scaffold with flexible connections; the pore is not a rigid cylinder with one universal channel diameter. Petrovic et al. (2022).
  • Passive passage slows gradually with reporter size in living budding yeast; a fixed 60 kDa exclusion rule misstates those measurements. Timney et al. (2016).
  • Interphase pore insertion was captured as inner-membrane evagination and fusion with the outer membrane in mammalian cells. Otsuka et al. (2016).
  • Nucleoporin disease evidence must specify the alteration and model: a NUP98-HOXA9 fusion in transplanted mouse marrow is not evidence that every NPC defect causes cancer. Kroon et al. (2001).

1. Route and Scope

NPCs form protein-lined connections across the double nuclear membrane. Human structural analysis places an inner ring around the transport channel and outer scaffold rings on its two faces; cytoplasmic filaments and the nuclear basket are asymmetric additions. Bley et al. (2022).

NPC-mediated exchange is the main subject here, but it is not an absolute description of every export route. Speese and colleagues reported nuclear-envelope budding of large ribonucleoprotein granules during synaptic Wnt signaling in Drosophila larval muscle. That specialized result does not establish an equivalent bulk export route in every human cell. Speese et al. (2012).

This study has a publisher erratum (2012). The full correction text was unavailable for this audit; affected figure panels and any conclusion impact remain unverified. This summary reports the study's bounded finding and does not validate its corrected figures.

2. Structural Architecture and Dimensions

What the structural models resolve

Biochemical reconstitution, crystallography and cryo-electron microscopy, combined with docking into tomographic maps, produced a near-atomic composite model of the human and fungal linker-scaffold. Eight relatively rigid inner-ring spokes are joined by flexible linkers, accommodating channel constriction and dilation. A composite model integrates several experiments; it is not one atomic-resolution image of an entire functioning pore. Petrovic et al. (2022).

The human framework is commonly described as approximately 110 MDa, with roughly 1,000 protein copies from about 34 nucleoporin types. These are approximate human-model composition estimates rather than a count for every vertebrate nucleus. Bley et al. (2022). Targeted proteomic comparisons across five human cell lines found differences in nucleoporin abundance, supporting context-dependent NPC composition. Super-resolution counting separately calibrated Nup107 copy number in an engineered HEK293 line. Ori et al. (2013).

Why a single diameter is insufficient

Eibauer and colleagues reconstructed NPCs from isolated Xenopus laevis oocyte nuclear envelopes by cryo-electron tomography and subtomogram averaging, reaching about 20 angstrom resolution in the most stable regions. Their channel-density interpretation is a proposed structural gating model, not a direct measurement of one universal passive size threshold. Eibauer et al. (2015). Constricted and dilated scaffold models further make a fixed channel diameter unsuitable as a universal transport rule. Petrovic et al. (2022).

3. Scaffold, Linker and FG Nucleoporins

The human inner ring contains scaffold layers involving NUP155, NUP93 and NUP188/NUP205, with the NUP54-NUP58-NUP62 channel complex toward the transport channel. NUP53, NUP98 and the linker region of NUP93 connect scaffold elements. This structural assignment comes from human/fungal biochemical and structural analysis, including structure-guided yeast tests. Petrovic et al. (2022).

Y-shaped coat complexes form the outer-ring scaffold. At the cytoplasmic face, human and fungal reconstitution resolved a cytoplasmic filament complex, while human-cell localization and depletion experiments constrained NUP358 attachment and function. Components with similar names should not be assumed to have identical anchoring mechanisms across species. Bley et al. (2022).

Phenylalanine-glycine (FG) repeat domains are flexible, largely disordered parts of nucleoporins. Yeast FG-domain deletion and reporter-permeability experiments show that different FG subsets contribute differently to passive resistance. Those measurements support selective, heterogeneous barrier behavior; they do not prove that every FG domain has one identical gel or brush organization in a living pore. Timney et al. (2016).

4. Passive Permeability: Rates and Experimental Context

Timney and colleagues followed nuclear-cytoplasmic reporter equilibration by fluorescence recovery after photobleaching in living Saccharomyces cerevisiae. GFP-based reporters spanning 27-67 kDa crossed with progressively slower kinetics rather than a sharp exclusion boundary. A 67 kDa reporter still equilibrated on a minutes timescale. These are reporter and yeast measurements, not a universal schedule for all human proteins. Timney et al. (2016).

Mohr and colleagues measured fluorescent probe uptake into digitonin-permeabilized HeLa nuclei and checked nuclear integrity after the diffusion measurements. Passage depended strongly on probe size, and adding transport receptors or transport inhibitors changed passive permeability. Permeabilization removes soluble cellular components, so these assays should not be equated directly with living-yeast measurements. Mohr et al. (2009).

A useful transport claim therefore identifies the probe, binding or receptor conditions, cell system and observation time. Molecular mass alone cannot establish whether a particular cargo will equilibrate within the relevant interval.

5. Transport Mechanisms and Ran

Ran nucleotide state regulates particular import and export pathways. Izaurralde and colleagues reported that nuclear injection of Ran-system regulators or a Ran mutant inhibited specific import and export reporters; RanGTP also released an M9 signal from transportin. This statement follows the primary abstract and indexed oocyte experiment metadata; detailed full-text methods were not retrieved in this audit. Izaurralde et al. (1997).

An important distinct mechanism is messenger-RNP remodeling. In vitro Dbp5 reconstitution and yeast genetic experiments showed that Nup159 promotes ADP release and Gle1-IP6 promotes ATP loading, supporting a model of repeated Dbp5 remodeling cycles at the cytoplasmic pore face. This is an ATPase mechanism and does not justify labeling every NPC transport event as Ran-powered. The retained details are explicit in the primary abstract; full-text methods were not retrieved. Noble et al. (2011).

6. Interphase Assembly

Correlative live-cell imaging, electron tomography and super-resolution microscopy captured NPC assembly intermediates in mammalian cells, principally staged HeLa cells, with additional observations in U2OS and normal rat kidney cells. The inner nuclear membrane evaginated outward, its intermediates enlarged, and fusion with the outer membrane completed the pore opening. Nup107 appeared before the cytoplasmic component Nup358. Otsuka et al. (2016).

This evidence addresses insertion into an already enclosed interphase nucleus. It should not be substituted for a full account of postmitotic envelope reassembly or used to assign one assembly rate to every cell type.

7. Disease and Aging: Specific Experiments

A defined leukemogenic fusion in a mouse model

Kroon and colleagues retrovirally expressed NUP98-HOXA9 in mouse bone marrow and transplanted the cells into recipients. The mice developed a myeloproliferative disorder that progressed to acute myeloid leukemia; co-expression of Meis1 accelerated progression. These findings concern an engineered fusion and animal transplantation model, not a clinical treatment or a demonstration that ordinary pore transport failure explains all leukemia. This account is restricted to the verified primary abstract. Kroon et al. (2001).

Age-related permeability in an experimental system

D'Angelo and colleagues compared nuclei from young (3-month) and old (28-month) rat brains and observed an age-related loss of exclusion of 70 kDa dextran, while 500 kDa dextran remained excluded. Their worm, differentiated-cell and rat experiments also linked long-lived scaffold components and pore deterioration. This is evidence from those models, not validation of a human neurodegenerative diagnosis or a universal claim that all NPC components never turn over. D'Angelo et al. (2009).

Evidence and Clinical Limits

The studies above answer different questions: structural organization, probe permeability, molecular remodeling, assembly intermediates or a particular animal phenotype. Their results should retain those experimental boundaries. A proposed scaffold arrangement, a reporter transport effect and an animal leukemia phenotype are not interchangeable measures of NPC function.

No diagnostic-accuracy estimate, validated NPC imaging biomarker, or patient treatment benefit is established by this page. Clinical conclusions would require condition-specific human evidence. The bibliography identifies the evidence used here; the linked long-form draft remains separately unreviewed.

Key Terms

Nucleoporins
Protein components of the NPC scaffold, channel or asymmetric structures. Bley et al. (2022).
FG repeats
Phenylalanine-glycine repeat regions involved in selective permeability. Timney et al. (2016).
Transport receptors
Factors such as importin beta that participate in receptor-mediated passage and can alter passive permeability in an assay. Mohr et al. (2009).
Ran nucleotide state
A regulator of particular import/export pathways; it does not describe every transport mechanism. Izaurralde et al. (1997); Noble et al. (2011).

Evidence at a Glance

  • Architecture: eight-spoke scaffold with flexible interconnections. Petrovic et al. (2022).
  • Composition: can vary with cell context. Ori et al. (2013).
  • Passive permeability: gradual size-dependent slowing in tested yeast reporters. Timney et al. (2016).
  • Assembly: interphase membrane intermediates captured by correlative microscopy. Otsuka et al. (2016).
